# 13 — Paracelsus: The Star in Man

**Theophrastus von Hohenheim**, called **Paracelsus** (born c. **1493/94**, Einsiedeln, Switzerland; died **24 September 1541**, Salzburg). Swiss-German physician, alchemist, lay theologian and natural philosopher. He reformed medicine (chemical remedies, toxicology, "the dose makes the poison") and was a major source for Renaissance **medical astrology** and Hermetic cosmology.

Most of his works were **published posthumously** (1560s–1590s). The standard early collected edition is **Johannes Huser's** (Basel, 1589–91). Modern critical edition: **Karl Sudhoff** (1922–33). His vocabulary is idiosyncratic and inconsistent, so scholars warn against reading a single "system" into him.

## 1. Key works for astrology and cosmology

| Work | Date (written / printed) | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| ***Volumen Paramirum*** | c. 1520 / later | The **five entia** (causes) of disease, including the *ens astrale* |
| ***Opus Paramirum*** | 1531 | The **tria prima** (sulphur, mercury, salt); disease as chemical process |
| ***Liber de nymphis, sylphis, pygmaeis et salamandris*** | c. 1530s / 1566 | Elemental spirits |
| ***Astronomia Magna*** (= ***Philosophia Sagax***) | **1537–38 / 1571** (Frankfurt, Feyerabend) | His fullest cosmology: man as a union of an **elemental** and a **sidereal (astral)** body, plus a divine spirit; "natural astronomy" and the knowledge proper to each |
| ***Labyrinthus medicorum errantium*** | 1538 | Attack on book-learned physicians; nature (including the sky) as the physician's "book" |
| *Prognostications* (annual and the 1536 *Prognosticatio*) | 1530s | He did write prophetic almanac-style works, which is part of his ambivalence |

## 2. Core ideas

### 2.1 Microcosm and macrocosm
Man is the **microcosm**, a compressed copy of the **macrocosm** (the universe). Everything in the heavens, the elements and the minerals has an analogue in the human being. To know the patient, the physician must know the world, which is why astronomy, alchemy and "philosophy" (natural science) were for him the **pillars of medicine**, together with *virtue* (the physician's integrity). See *Paragranum*, 1530.

### 2.2 The "star in man" and the astral (sidereal) body
- Man has a visible **elemental body** (from the earth) and an invisible **sidereal or astral body** (from the firmament), which carries imagination, reason, art and skill. A divine **spirit/soul** comes from God.
- There is an **inner firmament** (*Gestirn*, *astrum*) in every person: "the star in man". The outer stars do not mechanically push the body. The inner stars **correspond** to the outer ones, and harmony or discord between them shapes health and temperament.
- Scholars (e.g. the *British Journal for the History of Science* papers on "Paracelsus's two-way astrology") stress that his "stars" are often **inner powers or virtues**, not only planets. Imagination is itself an "astral" force that can "conceive" disease or healing.

### 2.3 The five entia of disease (*Volumen Paramirum*)
1. *Ens astrale*: influence of the stars, operating through the atmosphere or "M" (*mysterium*), a subtle medium. It is not the stars ruling directly.
2. *Ens veneni*: poisons and impurities from food and environment.
3. *Ens naturale*: the person's constitution, including their inner firmament.
4. *Ens spirituale*: the spirit, will and imagination (and others' spirits).
5. *Ens Dei*: God's providence, which is the ultimate cause.

The stars are only **one of five** causes, and not the master of the others.

### 2.4 The Archeus
The **Archeus** is an inner vital "alchemist", seated especially in the stomach. It separates nourishment from poison and governs growth and healing. Disease is in part the Archeus failing to do its work. (Van Helmont developed the idea further in the 17th century.)

### 2.5 Signatures
Nature marks things with **signs** of their virtues: shape, colour and habitat point to medical use (e.g. lung-like lichen for lungs, *Euphrasia* for eyes). Paracelsus is a key early source. The doctrine was systematised by **Giambattista della Porta** (*Phytognomonica*, 1588), **Oswald Croll** (*De signaturis*, 1609) and **Jakob Böhme** (*De signatura rerum*, 1622). There is **no pharmacological basis** for signatures as a discovery method. Hits like willow for fevers are coincidences of folk use.

### 2.6 Tria prima
Every body is composed of **sulphur** (combustibility, soul), **mercury** (volatility, spirit) and **salt** (fixity, body). These are added to the four Aristotelian elements and laid the groundwork for iatrochemistry.

## 3. Planet–metal–organ correspondences

The classical seven-fold scheme, as transmitted in Paracelsian and later Hermetic medicine. Paracelsus uses it but does not give a single consistent table, and later Paracelsians standardised it.

| Planet | Metal | Organ (Paracelsian tradition) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ☉ Sun | Gold | **Heart** | Vital centre; gold as cordial (aurum potabile) |
| ☽ Moon | Silver | **Brain** | Cold and moist, tides of fluids; mental states |
| ☿ Mercury | Quicksilver | **Lungs** | Mercury salts used for syphilis (a Paracelsian innovation, toxic) |
| ♀ Venus | Copper | **Kidneys** | |
| ♂ Mars | Iron | **Gall bladder** | Iron for anaemia/"weak blood" later became mainstream |
| ♃ Jupiter | Tin | **Liver** | |
| ♄ Saturn | Lead | **Spleen** | Melancholy, black bile |

Some sources give different organ assignments (e.g. Mars–liver, Jupiter–lungs). Compare the zodiac "melothesia" (Aries = head … Pisces = feet) from Manilius and Ptolemy, which Paracelsus also inherited. See [01-how-astrology-works.md](01-how-astrology-works.md).

## 4. Stance toward astrology: "the wise man rules the stars"

Paracelsus was **ambivalent**:

- **Against fatalism.** He opposed the idea that birth stars fix one's fate. The maxim ***"Sapiens dominabitur astris"*** ("The wise man will rule the stars") comes from the pseudo-Ptolemaic *Centiloquium* tradition and was widely repeated. Paracelsus expresses the same view: the stars **incline**, they do not compel; a person who lives by wisdom and the spirit rises above "astral" determination, and only the "animal" man is ruled by the stars. Some writers attribute a line like "the stars compel the animal man; the wise man rules the stars" directly to him. Treat exact wording as **paraphrase** unless you can cite Huser/Sudhoff.
- **For a reformed "astronomy".** He rejected the astrology of the book-learned (complex horoscope rules from Arabic and Latin authorities) in favour of **experiential** knowledge of correspondences. A physician should still know the heavens to understand the patient, timing and remedies.
- **Practice.** He wrote prognostications and used celestial timing, so his practice was less radical than his rhetoric. Later popular claims, including one repeated by Osho, that he refused to treat anyone without first studying the birth chart are **not well documented** in his own works. See [14-osho.md](14-osho.md).

## 5. Iatromathematics: astrological medicine

- **Iatromathematics** (Greek *iatros*, physician, + *mathēmatikos*, astrologer) is the medieval and Renaissance discipline that combined astrology with medicine. It used **decumbiture** (a chart for the moment the patient took to bed), critical days by lunar phases, astrological timing of **bloodletting** and herb-gathering, and planetary herbal rulerships.
- Precursors and contemporaries: Galen's critical days, Ibn Ezra, Pietro d'Abano, **Marsilio Ficino** (*De vita libri tres*, 1489).
- Successors: Paracelsians (Croll, Gerhard Dorn, Robert Fludd, Van Helmont) and English astrological physicians such as **Nicholas Culpeper** (*Complete Herbal*, 1653; *Astrological Judgement of Diseases*, 1655).
- Medical astrology declined in academic medicine in the 17th–18th centuries but survives in modern esoteric practice (e.g. Rudolf Steiner's anthroposophical medicine, which uses planetary metals).

## 6. Later influence

- **Rosicrucianism and Hermeticism** (17th c.): Fludd's macrocosm–microcosm diagrams.
- **Theosophy.** Franz Hartmann, *The Life of Paracelsus* (1887), made "the astral body" a household term.
- **Anthroposophy.** Steiner uses the planet–metal–organ table. Weleda and Wala make planetary-metal remedies.
- **C. G. Jung.**
  - *"Paracelsus"* (1929 lecture, Einsiedeln) and *"Paracelsus the Physician"* (1941). Both are in **Collected Works vol. 15**, *The Spirit in Man, Art, and Literature*.
  - ***"Paracelsus as a Spiritual Phenomenon"*** (1942, *Paracelsica*). In **CW vol. 13**, *Alchemical Studies*. Jung reads Paracelsus's inner firmament, *lumen naturae* (light of nature) and the Archeus as symbols of the unconscious and of individuation. This psychological re-reading feeds directly into Jung's interest in astrology as a projection of psychic contents and in synchronicity. See [15-other-cycle-astrology-thinkers.md](15-other-cycle-astrology-thinkers.md#jung).
- **Medicine proper.** Chemical pharmacology, toxicology (dose–response), occupational disease (*On the Miners' Sickness*, 1533/1567). These lasting contributions have nothing to do with astrology.

## 7. Relevance to this project
- Paracelsus is the conceptual root of the **"resonance, not compulsion"** reading of astrology: the inner state mediates outer influences. Osho's "essential vs. changeable" layers and Jungian psychological astrology both descend from this idea.
- The planet–metal–organ table is useful as a **lookup table** for interpretive output. Label it as tradition, not medical advice.
